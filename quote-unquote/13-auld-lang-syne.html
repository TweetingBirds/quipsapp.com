Burns Said He Didn't Write "Auld Lang Syne"

He credited an old man nobody can find. The tune we sing tonight isn't the one it was printed with.

By Nate

Tonight, at midnight, a lot of people are going to sing a song whose words they half know, in a language most of them don't speak, about a question they never quite answer.

It's the last issue of the year, so it had to be "Auld Lang Syne." And the man who gave it to us kept telling people it wasn't his.

"Should auld acquaintance be forgot,
And never brought to mind?
Should auld acquaintance be forgot,
And auld lang syne!" — Robert Burns, "Auld lang syne," The Scots Musical Museum, vol. 5 (1796)

The Story Behind It

In December 1788, Burns wrote to his friend Frances Dunlop. He'd been thinking about old friendships, and asked her, "is not the Scotch phrase, 'Auld lang syne,' exceedingly expressive? There is an old song and tune which has often thrilled through my soul."1 He copied out the verses for her and added:1

"Light be the turf on the breast of the heaven-inspired poet who composed this glorious fragment."

Five years later he sent the song to the music publisher George Thomson and went further. "The air is but mediocre," he wrote, "but the following song, the old song of the olden times, and which has never been in print, nor even in manuscript, until I took it down from an old man's singing, is enough to recommend any air."2

That's a very modest poet. It's also not quite true.

Back to the Source

The opening line really is older than Burns. A song attributed to the court poet Sir Robert Aytoun, who died in 1638, begins "Should auld acquaintance be forgot, / And never thought upon," and it was printed in James Watson's Choice Collection of Scots Poems in 1711, a book Burns knew.3 So "never been in print" was wrong.

But the parts people actually sing are mostly Burns. The Library of Congress's folklorist Stephen Winick points out that "never brought to mind" and moving "we'll tak a cup o' kindness yet" into the chorus came from him.3 And the old man? As Kirsteen McCue of the University of Glasgow puts it, there's "no evidence of who this man might have been," and Burns "may even have fabricated the story."4

The phrase itself, by the way, translates roughly as "old time's sake."4

The Wrinkle Worth Knowing

The words were first printed in James Johnson's Scots Musical Museum, in the fifth volume, as song number 413.5 Burns died in 1796, the year it came out.3, 4

Two things on that page surprised me. The first is the fourth line. It's "And auld lang syne!", the version most of us sing - while the copy Burns sent Mrs. Dunlop reads "And days o' lang syne?"1, 5 The other is a footnote engraved at the bottom of the page: "Some Sing Kiss in place of Cup."5 A cup o' kindness, or a kiss o' kindness. Even in 1796 people couldn't agree on the words.

The tune wasn't settled either. The Museum set the song to a slow, haunting melody. Thomson, after Burns died, matched it to a livelier fiddle tune known as "The Miller's Wedding," and published it that way in 1799.4 That's the tune you'll hear tonight. Burns didn't live to hear what Thomson did with it.

Why It Matters Today

Burns could have taken credit for this song. Instead he gave it to an old man and a nameless "heaven-inspired poet," and in a way he was right. He took a line people already sang and finished it, and then other people changed the tune and the words after him. It's a song about remembering where things came from, and its own history is a little blurry.

Maybe that's why it works at midnight. Nobody owns it, so everyone can sing it, even the people who only know the first verse.
