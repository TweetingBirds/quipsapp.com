Yes, Virginia, Someone Wrote That

The most famous editorial ever printed ran unsigned in the third column. Its author got his name on it only after he died.

By Nate

Christmas is eight days away, so this week's quote was the obvious one. Almost everybody knows the first five words.

Far fewer people know who wrote them. That's not their fault. The newspaper kept it a secret on purpose, and only told the world after the writer had died.

"Yes, Virginia, there is a Santa Claus. He exists as certainly as love and generosity and devotion exist, and you know that they abound and give to your life its highest beauty and joy." — Francis Pharcellus Church, "Is There a Santa Claus?", The Sun (New York), September 21, 1897

The Story Behind It

In 1897 an eight-year-old girl named Virginia O'Hanlon wrote to The Sun, a New York daily. Her letter is short enough to quote whole:1

"I am eight years old. Some of my little friends say there is no Santa Claus. Papa says 'If you see it in the Sun it's so.' Please tell me the truth, is there a Santa Claus?"

She signed it with her name and her address, 115 West Ninety-Fifth Street.

The Sun printed the letter and its answer on September 21, 1897. Not in December. September. The paper introduced it by saying it took "pleasure in answering at once and thus prominently."1 The prominence was relative: it ran in the third and last column of editorials that day.2

The answer is 416 words long.2 It starts by telling Virginia her little friends "have been affected by the skepticism of a skeptical age," and it ends with a promise: "A thousand years from now, Virginia, nay, ten times ten thousand years from now, he will continue to make glad the heart of childhood."1

The Man Behind the Editorial

The answer was unsigned, because every editorial in The Sun was. The paper treated the anonymity of its editorial writers as absolute.1

The writer was Francis Pharcellus Church, a veteran Sun man whose work for the paper stretched across about forty years. With his brother William he had founded the Army and Navy Journal during the Civil War and, in 1866, The Galaxy, a literary magazine that published early work by Henry James.3

When Church died on April 11, 1906, The Sun did something it had never done. It announced that he was the author. Frank M. O'Brien, who wrote the paper's history in 1918, says Church "stands alone as the writer in whose case the Sun broke its rule."1

So for more than eight years, what O'Brien calls "the most popular editorial article ever written" had no name on it. One newspaper that reprinted it that December even credited the wrong man.2

What Happened to the Letter

Virginia grew up and kept getting mail about her letter for the rest of her life. She died in 1971.2

And the letter itself survived. In 1997 her great-granddaughter brought it to Antiques Roadshow in Phoenix. The appraiser, Kathleen Guzman, called it "the original letter when she was eight years old" and valued it at $20,000 to $30,000 at auction. When the segment aired again in 2012, the estimate went up to $30,000 to $50,000.4

Why It Matters Today

It's easy to remember "Yes, Virginia" as a sweet, slightly corny bit of Christmas copy. Read the whole thing and it's stranger and better than that. Church was a grown man arguing, in a serious newspaper, that "the most real things in the world are those that neither children nor men can see."1

He made his case without his name, and the paper only gave it back to him when he couldn't enjoy it. That's the quiet irony of what may be the most famous unsigned writing in American journalism. It's also a good reason to keep his name attached to it now.
